Overture

Opera was invented to resurrect something that had been lost. The Florentine Camerata, attempting to revive the drama of ancient Greece, accidentally created the most expensive, most demanding and most emotionally overwhelming art form the world has ever seen. It has been in crisis ever since. It has also never stopped.

The Voice

The trained operatic voice is one of the rarest and most fragile instruments in existence. It takes a decade to form, a lifetime to master, and a single illness to silence. This section examines how the voice is made — and what the industry is doing to protect it.

◆

Formation

The voice begins to mature in adolescence but rarely reaches full operatic capability before the late twenties. Most conservatoires now delay heavy repertoire training until singers are physiologically ready — a shift from the 20th-century practice of pushing voices young and fast.

◆

Range and Register

The operatic Fach system classifies voices into approximately twenty categories — from the lyric soprano to the Heldentenor to the basso profondo. A singer's Fach determines their repertoire, their career arc, and often the roles that will define them.

◆

Endurance

A dramatic soprano singing Brünnhilde in Wagner's Ring Cycle must sustain three to four hours of vocally demanding performance across four evenings. The physical demands are comparable to an elite athlete. Rest, hydration and repertoire management are not optional — they are the career.

◆

Protection

The ROH now employs a resident voice health team — laryngologists, speech therapists and physiotherapists — embedded in the rehearsal process. Vocal fatigue indicators are tracked across the season. The days of singing through illness are ending.

Opera is the only art form in which a human being can make you weep simply by standing still and opening their mouth.

Cecilia Bartoli, mezzo-soprano

Who Comes

The question of who opera is for has haunted the form since its invention. The short answer — everyone — has never been self-evident in practice. This season, for the first time, the ROH published a full audience diversity report.

The median age of a first-time ROH visitor fell to 34 this season — the lowest recorded figure. The under-35 audience grew by 19% year-on-year, driven by the £10 Friday Rush ticket scheme and expanded school-visit programme. The over-65 audience remains the largest single cohort at 31% of all attendances.

48% of live audience members travelled from outside Greater London this season. International visitors — attending as part of a planned trip to the UK — accounted for 22% of Main Stage attendances, reflecting London's role as a global operatic destination. Regional touring reached 14 cities.

Audio description, captioning and British Sign Language-interpreted performances are now standard across all Main Stage productions. The ROH's relaxed performances — designed for neurodivergent audience members and those with sensory sensitivities — sold out every date this season, prompting an expansion to eight performances per season from 2026–27.

The Company

The voices behind this season.

Asmik Grigorian

Soprano

Armenian-Lithuanian soprano Asmik Grigorian made her ROH debut this season as Violetta in a new production of La Traviata. Her Salzburg Salome is already considered one of the defining operatic performances of the 21st century.

Artist profile

Gerald Finley

Baritone

Canadian baritone Gerald Finley returned to the ROH this season for his fifth Golaud in Pelléas et Mélisande, a role he has made definitive across three decades. He also premiered the title role in Antigone.

Artist profile

Simone Young

Chief Conductor

Australian conductor Simone Young became the first woman to conduct the Vienna Philharmonic and the first to conduct the complete Ring cycle at the Vienna State Opera. She begins her tenure as ROH Chief Conductor in the 2026–27 season.
